HeatSignal NYC · BioHack 2026 · Public Health & Epidemiology

Most heat ER visits in New York happen on days with no heat advisory.

Heat is New York's deadliest weather hazard. About 500 New Yorkers die early from hot weather each summer, and Black New Yorkers die of heat stress at twice the rate of white New Yorkers.1 The city issues a heat advisory when the heat index reaches 100°F, or 95°F two days in a row.2 We checked that rule against ten summers of the Health Department's daily ER records for heat illness.3 HeatSignal adds a lower Heat Watch tier based on expected ER visits and sends low-cost help to the neighborhoods most likely to need it.

    4 · Evidence

    Ten summers, tested one at a time

    Leave-one-summer-out cross-validation: each summer is scored by a model trained only on the other nine. A high-burden day is one in the top 10% of summer days (13 or more heat ER visits).

    Is the gain real?

    • Resampling whole summers 2,000 times, the advisory catches of bad days and HeatSignal (95% intervals). The gain is percentage points.
    • Across heat-illness episodes (runs of bad days), a warning was in place from the first bad day in with the advisory and with HeatSignal.
    • About more heat ER visits fell on warned days over ten summers, roughly 100 a summer.
    • Forecasts are well calibrated. Days the model expected to be bad were about that bad:

    What already exists, and what's new

    • NWS heat advisory. A fixed heat-index rule (95°F two days, or 100°F).2 Its 95 to 100°F range matches a 2010 Health Department study of 1997 to 2006 deaths, which advised re-checking it with recent data.9 We did, and it is the baseline we test against.
    • NWS HeatRisk (with CDC) rates heat 0 to 4 from local climate, time of year and duration.5 It is national. It is not tuned to New York's own ER visits, and it does not say where to send help.
    • Be a Buddy pairs volunteers with at-risk neighbors in heat-vulnerable neighborhoods.6 HeatSignal tells programs like this which days to call, and gives them a ranked list of neighborhoods.
    • Sub-advisory triggers already exist. Con Edison pauses residential shutoffs when the heat index is forecast to reach 90°F.7 A Heat Watch fits city practice.
    • What's new here: a trigger set from the city's own heat-illness ER data, stated in expected patients and tested summer by summer, with action costs matched to how certain the warning is.

    How the model works

    • Daily heat ER visits come from syndromic surveillance, which covers every ER in the city. Heat ER visits track excess deaths in NYC.38
    • Poisson regression of daily heat ER visits on the day's max heat index, with extra slope above 85, 90 and 95°F.
    • Also uses yesterday's heat, consecutive hot days, weekends, early-season heat (before the summer's 5th day at 90°F+), and ER visits two days earlier. That is the latest count the city has when it issues a warning a day ahead.
    • Heat Watch fires when expected visits reach the summer's top 13% of days, about 20 days a summer. Every advisory day is also a Watch day.
    • Forecast check: adding ±2.5°F of random forecast error to every day still catches of high-burden days.

    What it can't do yet

    • The ER counts are suspected heat illness from chief complaints and diagnosis codes. They miss people who never reach an ER, and heat deaths at home.
    • Weather comes from one station (LaGuardia). Street-level heat in the South Bronx can run hotter.
    • A plain lower heat-index threshold catches almost as many bad days (). Most of the gain comes from warning at the temperatures where people actually get sick. The model adds expected visits, early-season adjustment and fewer false alarms.
    • We have citywide daily counts only. Neighborhood targeting uses the vulnerability index, not neighborhood ER forecasts.
    • It underestimates the worst multi-day early heat waves. On June 25, 2025, the fourth day of a heat wave that killed 19 people, it expected 31 ER visits. The real count was 141.1 Both systems were already warning that day, but the size of the surge was missed.
